## Model 1 · Paragraph: "Cashless Bangladesh" (148 words)

A cashless economy is one in which payments are made through digital channels — cards, mobile wallets, internet banking and QR codes — rather than physical notes and coins. Bangladesh has moved quickly in this direction. Mobile financial services such as bKash, Nagad and Rocket now reach tens of millions of users, while the National Payment Switch and Bangla QR have made small digital payments common even in rural markets. The benefits are real: transactions become faster, records become traceable, and the cost of handling cash falls for both banks and customers. Yet the transition is incomplete. Poor internet coverage, low digital literacy, fraud and the dependence of informal workers on cash still exclude a large part of the population. A genuinely cashless Bangladesh will therefore require reliable connectivity, strong consumer protection and financial education alongside new technology. The goal should not be to remove cash overnight, but to make digital payment so safe and simple that people choose it.

## Model 2 · Paragraph: "The Role of a Cash Officer in a Bank" (147 words)

A cash officer is the face of a bank at its most practical level. The role involves receiving deposits, paying out withdrawals, verifying signatures and instruments, managing the cash drawer, and balancing the day's transactions before closing. Accuracy matters more than speed: a single note counted twice or a signature checked carelessly can create a loss that the officer may be asked to make good. Beyond arithmetic, the job is also about trust. Customers who are nervous about their savings, unfamiliar with forms, or unsure about a new card depend on the officer to explain things patiently and honestly. A good cash officer therefore combines three qualities — precision, honesty and courtesy. Technology is changing the role, with cash recycling machines and digital transfers reducing routine counting, but it cannot replace the judgement and integrity that the position demands.

## Model 3 · Essay: "Financial Inclusion and National Development" (334 words)

Financial inclusion means ensuring that every adult, including the poor and those in remote areas, has access to useful and affordable financial services — savings, credit, insurance and payments. It is not a welfare slogan; it is an economic strategy. A country that leaves half its population outside the formal financial system is, in effect, running on half its capital.

The first contribution of financial inclusion is to mobilise savings. When people keep money at home, it sits idle. When they keep it in a bank or a mobile wallet, it becomes available for lending to farmers, traders and small manufacturers. Bangladesh has shown how quickly this can happen: mobile financial services and agent banking have brought millions of previously unbanked people into the system within a decade, often through a shop within walking distance rather than a branch in a distant town.

Second, inclusion reduces vulnerability. A family with an insurance policy or a small emergency loan can survive a flood, an illness or a sudden job loss without selling its assets or borrowing from a moneylender at ruinous interest. This stability supports consumption, keeps children in school and protects small businesses.

Third, inclusion supports the formal economy. Digital payments leave a record. A record allows a shopkeeper to build a credit history and obtain a loan that a bank would never have given against cash alone. Over time, this shifts economic activity from the informal to the formal sector and widens the tax base.

None of this happens automatically. Financial inclusion requires trust, and trust requires regulation: clear rules on mobile money, protection against fraud, simple complaint mechanisms and literacy programmes. Without these, inclusion can become exploitation.

In conclusion, financial inclusion is one of the cheapest development policies available. It converts idle savings into investment, protects households from shocks and brings informal activity into the open. For Bangladesh, the task now is not to celebrate the number of accounts opened but to ensure that those accounts are used, trusted and safe.

## Model 4 · Essay: "Inflation and the Common People of Bangladesh" (327 words)

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level. For a salaried family in Dhaka, it is not an abstract statistic; it is the difference between the same monthly income buying a full basket of rice, oil and vegetables last year and a smaller one this year.

The burden of inflation is never shared equally. It falls hardest on fixed-income earners, day labourers and low-paid workers, whose wages adjust slowly or not at all. A rise in food prices consumes a larger share of a poor household's budget than of a rich one, so the same inflation rate can mean a mild inconvenience for one family and a skipped meal for another. Bangladesh's recent inflation, driven largely by food and fuel costs, has demonstrated this clearly.

There are several causes. Supply disruptions raise the cost of imports and transport. A depreciating taka makes imported fuel and fertiliser more expensive. Weak domestic production and supply-chain syndication keep prices high even when global prices ease. Expansionary government spending and borrowing can add demand-side pressure. Because much of this is cost-push rather than demand-pull, raising interest rates alone cannot solve it.

The policy response therefore has to be broader than monetary tightening. Bangladesh Bank's higher policy rate helps anchor expectations, but it must be supported by measures that increase supply: agricultural support, better storage, smoother import procedures and genuine competition in retail markets. Targeted subsidies and cash transfers can protect the poorest without distorting prices for everyone. Wage boards and employers also have a role in protecting real incomes.

In conclusion, inflation is a macroeconomic problem with a very personal cost. Controlling it requires coordination between monetary, fiscal and supply-side policy, and a clear focus on the households that can least afford to wait. Price stability is not merely a central-bank target; it is a condition for social stability.

## Model 5 · Argument: "Cash will disappear by 2030. Do you agree?" (238 words)

The prediction that cash will disappear by 2030 is attractive to anyone who has queued at an ATM, but it confuses a trend with a deadline. I largely disagree.

The case for disappearance is real. Digital payments in Bangladesh have grown extraordinarily, and for urban, salaried and younger consumers, cash is already optional. Cards, mobile wallets and QR codes are faster, traceable and cheaper to process. Governments like the visibility that digital payments give them, and banks like the reduction in handling costs.

However, three obstacles make a complete disappearance by 2030 implausible. First, infrastructure. A cashless economy requires electricity and internet coverage that are still unreliable in parts of rural Bangladesh and in many developing countries. Second, inclusion. Millions of people — the elderly, the very poor, informal workers and those without identity documents — depend on cash and would be excluded by its removal. Third, trust. When a network fails, a wallet is frozen or a fraud occurs, cash is the fallback that still works.

The more likely outcome is not disappearance but a decline in the *share* of transactions made in cash, with cash retained for small, rural and emergency payments. Sweden and Norway, among the most digitally advanced societies, still protect the right to use cash for exactly these reasons.

In conclusion, cash will become less common but not extinct by 2030. A responsible policy is to make digital payments excellent while guaranteeing that no citizen is cut off from the economy for lacking a smartphone.

## Model 6 · Critical writing: "Society should rely on consumer action rather than government regulation to make companies ethical. Discuss." (241 words)

The statement proposes that boycotts and consumer choice are a better tool than law for forcing companies to behave ethically. I partly agree that consumer action matters, but I disagree that it should be the primary instrument.

Consumer pressure has genuine power. A boycott damages revenue and reputation, and companies respond quickly when their brand is at risk. Campaigns against child labour and sweatshop conditions have changed supply chains that regulation had failed to reach. In that sense, consumer action can move faster than legislation.

Yet it has two structural weaknesses. The first is information. Consumers cannot verify how a product was made, so their choices are only as good as the labels and journalism available to them. The second is inequality. Ethical products are often more expensive, which means that the poorest consumers cannot afford to act on their principles. Consumer action therefore tends to reflect the preferences of the affluent while leaving the practices that harm the poor untouched.

Regulation does not depend on individual wealth or awareness. A law on minimum wages, factory safety or pollution applies to every firm and carries a penalty for breach. It also solves the collective-action problem: an individual consumer cannot police a global supply chain, but a regulator can.

The most effective model combines the two. Consumer pressure identifies problems and creates political momentum; regulation turns that momentum into enforceable standards. Neither is sufficient alone, but law must be the foundation, because ethics that depend on the buyer's budget are not ethics at all.

## Model 9 · Precise writing (example)

**Original passage (about 90 words):**
> It is a matter of great regret that most of the students of our country are not aware of the importance of time. They waste their valuable time in idle talk and in activities that bring no benefit. As a result, they cannot complete their studies in time and often fail to build a good career. Time, once lost, never comes back. It is therefore essential that students are taught from an early age to use their time properly and to set clear priorities in life.

**Precise version (about 30 words):**
> Most students in Bangladesh waste valuable time on unproductive activities, which harms their studies and careers. Since lost time cannot be recovered, students should be taught early to manage time and set priorities.

**Rule:** keep the meaning, cut the adjectives, remove repetition, use your own words, aim for one-third of the original length.

## Model 10 · Written math — three worked answers

### (a) Set problem

> In a college of 100 students, 60% took Finance, 45% took Marketing and 45 students took Operations. 10 students took all three courses and 10 took none. How many took exactly two courses?

**Given:** n(T) = 100, n(F) = 60, n(M) = 45, n(O) = 45, n(F∩M∩O) = 10, none = 10.

**Step 1 — students taking at least one:**
n(F∪M∪O) = 100 − 10 = 90.

**Step 2 — apply the inclusion–exclusion formula:**
n(F∪M∪O) = n(F) + n(M) + n(O) − [n(F∩M) + n(M∩O) + n(O∩F)] + n(F∩M∩O)
90 = 60 + 45 + 45 − S + 10
90 = 160 − S
S = 70

**Step 3 — exactly two:**
Exactly two = S − 3 × n(F∩M∩O) = 70 − 30 = **40**

**Answer: 40 students.**

### (b) Tree-planting geometry

> A rectangular plot is 90 ft long and 66 ft wide, surrounded by a wall. Trees are planted leaving 5 ft free from the wall on all four sides, with 4 ft between trees in every row and column. What is the maximum number of trees?

**Step 1 — usable dimensions:**
Length = 90 − (5 + 5) = 80 ft
Width = 66 − (5 + 5) = 56 ft

**Step 2 — number of trees per row and column (add 1 because both ends are planted):**
Rows = (80 ÷ 4) + 1 = 20 + 1 = 21
Columns = (56 ÷ 4) + 1 = 14 + 1 = 15

**Step 3 — total:**
21 × 15 = **315**

**Answer: 315 trees.**

### (c) Profit and loss

> The percentage profit earned by selling an item for Tk 1,920 equals the percentage loss incurred by selling it for Tk 1,280. At what price should it be sold to earn a 25% profit?

**Step 1 — let CP = x.**
Profit% = (1920 − x)/x × 100
Loss% = (x − 1280)/x × 100

**Step 2 — set them equal (the ×100 and ÷x cancel):**
1920 − x = x − 1280
2x = 3200
x = 1600

**Step 3 — selling price for 25% profit:**
SP = 1600 × 1.25 = **Tk 2,000**

**Answer: Tk 2,000.**
